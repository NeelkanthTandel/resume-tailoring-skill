## 4. Honest fit assessment

This is a **stretch application**, and it is worth being clear about that rather than papering over it.

The JD reads mid-to-senior: leading complex design challenges, proposing and executing projects across products, creating primitives that other people build on. The source resume shows roughly two years since graduation — one agency design role plus two internships — with work described as contributing and incorporating senior feedback. Wording cannot close that distance, and no honest resume will.

What *can* close it, and what this resume is built to lead with:

- **The engineering background is genuinely rare and directly requested.** Most product design applicants cannot say they shipped an Angular/.NET/SQL Server application. Supabase asked for exactly this and it is the reason this application is worth submitting.
- **Supabase hires on demonstrated craft and shipping, not years.** They build in public and say "prototype and show ideas rather than talk about them." The portfolio and any public work will outweigh the resume here.

**Blunt recommendation:** the highest-leverage hours are not in further resume wording. They are in (a) filling the Projects gap in §5 and (b) shipping one small, public, Supabase-shaped thing before applying. A working prototype built on Supabase, with a short write-up of the design decisions, is worth more to this specific hiring team than any bullet rewrite.

## 5. What would materially strengthen this — in priority order

**1. Add a Projects section. Highest impact by a wide margin.**
The JD explicitly names "actively building things outside of work; side projects, personal experiments; you're curious and you ship" — and the source resume is silent on it. This is the single largest gap. Drop-in template:

```markdown
## Projects

**{Project name}** | [Live]({url}) · [GitHub]({url}) · [Case study]({url})
- {What it does and who it's for, in one line.}
- {The design decision you had to reason through, and what you chose.}
- {What you built it with — name the stack, especially if you wrote the code.}
```

Two projects beats one; one beats none. Anything counts if you designed *and* shipped it: a tool you built for yourself, a redesign you took all the way to working code, an AI-assisted build. Bonus if it uses Supabase.

**2. Ship one Supabase-flavoured thing before applying.**
Concrete options, roughly a weekend each: build a small app on Supabase and write up the onboarding friction you hit; take one screen of the Supabase dashboard, redesign it, and build the redesign as a working prototype; or contribute a fix to `supabase/supabase` (it's open source — even a small UI or docs contribution is a real signal to this team).

**3. Put numbers on the Mobio work.**
Currently there is not a single metric in the resume. Answers to any of these become bullets: How many components ended up in the library, and how many projects reused them? How many users or screens did the products you designed have? Did any usability finding produce a measurable change — fewer support tickets, fewer steps, faster completion, higher conversion? How long did a typical project take end to end?

**4. Give the research bullet substance.**
What methods — user interviews, session recordings, analytics, competitive review, usability tests with real participants? How many people did you talk to? What did you change as a result? "Ran 6 moderated usability sessions and cut a 5-step flow to 3" is worth ten of the current bullet.

**5. Confirm two inferred skills before sending.**
`Git` and `TypeScript` are in the skills list by reasonable inference (Angular is TypeScript-based; a professional dev internship implies version control) but neither is stated in the source resume. Remove either if it isn't accurate.

**6. Anything else undocumented.**
Freelance or volunteer design work, a design-systems effort you drove, hackathons, mentoring juniors, open-source contributions, writing, a Dribbble/Behance presence, developer-tool or B2B/SaaS products you've designed. Any of these change the resume.

## 6. Interview preparation

**Stories to have ready**
- *The primitive story.* One component or pattern you built that got reused elsewhere — how you decided its boundaries and what you deliberately left out. This is the JD's "product primitives" bullet and they will probe it.
- *The engineer conversation.* A time a developer said "this can't be built that way" — what you changed, and how your own coding background shaped the conversation.
- *Speed vs. craft.* A time you shipped something you knew wasn't perfect, and how you decided that was correct. The JD asks for this in its own words: "know when good enough is good enough."
- *Research changing your mind.* A design you altered because of what a user did rather than what you assumed.

**Questions to expect**
- "Walk me through something you designed *and* built."
- "How do you design for developers? What's different about that user?"
- "Show me a prototype." — have something clickable, and ideally something running.
- "What have you shipped outside work recently?" — see §5, item 1.

**Gaps to get ahead of**
- Seniority. Don't hide behind vague language; lead with what you've shipped and how fast you learn.
- No prior developer-tool product. Neutralize it by having used Supabase and having an opinion about its UX.
- Remote/async. If you've collaborated across time zones or documented work in writing, have that example ready.

**Do your homework on:** Supabase's public design system and its Radix-primitives foundation, the "How design works at Supabase" post on their blog, and the dashboard's move toward a SQL-first experience. Their design work is open source — you can read the actual components before the interview, which very few candidates will.
